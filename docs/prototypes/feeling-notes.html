Prototype · feelings picker

Notes on a Feeling

A few words of your own against a single word — only about the meeting, not about him — written in a drawer the screen makes room for, and gone again the moment you clear the box. Offered by the line under the definition, opened by a key. Everything below is live: click into a demo, mark a word, and press N.

The drawer

The screen parks and makes room

The picker pushes itself up, fades, and dissolves into the top edge; a drawer comes in off the bottom carrying the word and a box that starts one line tall and grows to five. The modal's own height never changes, so nothing behind it moves — and because the drawer grows upwards, the box stays where the thumbs and the software keyboard already are.

While the picker has focus
NWrite a note on this word, or open the one already there. Focus moves into the box.
EnterKeep it. The drawer closes and the note is in the card, under the definition. In the grid that also marks the word; in the walk the card is still there, still unanswered.
Shift EnterA new line. The box grows under it, and the drawer climbs to make room.
Enter on emptyDeletes the note. Clearing the box is the delete; there is no second control for it.
Esc or ✕Puts the box back the way it was found and closes the drawer. The drawer is the screen on top, so both leave it and neither leaves the category.
← →In the walk they answer — except inside the box, where they are the caret.
Everything elseInert while the drawer is open: the words, the answers, and the button row still in view below it. There is nothing to do but finish the note or drop it.

A note only exists on a word that is picked. There is nothing a note on an unpicked feeling could mean and nowhere for it to be written. So the rule runs both ways: keeping a note marks the word in the grid, and unmarking a word takes its note with it. In the walk, where the card is answered rather than marked, the note is part of the answer and not a substitute for it: Enter puts it in the card and leaves the card where it was, → keeps the word and the note together, and ← discards the word and everything written about it.

How it is offered

Marking a word offers the note

The line under the definition says what it holds: Press N to add a few words of your own on a marked word with nothing written on it, and the note itself — followed by Press N to edit — once there is. It appears only on a marked word, which ties it to the rule that makes a note legal: marking is what introduces notes, and sweeping across twenty-eight unmarked words offers nothing.

It must not look clickable, and in the grid it is not. To reach a target down there the pointer has to sweep across the words above it, and every word it crosses changes what the line is about — so the click you aimed at livid would land on outraged, or on nothing. Naming a key instead costs the pointer nothing: the word stays under the mouse while the other hand answers.

A phone has no hover to lose and no key to press, so there the same line is a target and says Tap to add. It is one @media (pointer: coarse) rule over one component; the switch below stands in for it.

Pointer
In the walk

On a phone the tap is the whole way in, so it is drawn as one. A finger never crosses the words on its way to the line — it travels through the air — so the word the line is about is the last one deliberately tapped and cannot drift under it. That is the rule about not looking clickable read the other way round: the affordance appears exactly where it can be used, as a dashed rule under the invitation, at a height a thumb can hit. There is no other route to reach it — tapping a marked word again unmarks it, and would take the note with it.

The walk is the exception, and can afford the click. A card is one word: the pointer crosses nothing on its way down, so there the line opens the drawer on either pointer. Without it a mouse in a walk would have no way to a note at all — the two answers are the only other targets on the screen.

What touch still owes an answer is not the tap but the keyboard it summons: it comes up over the bottom of the screen, which is exactly where the drawer is anchored. The drawer will have to sit above it — the visual viewport, not the layout one, is what knows where it ends — and that is a real piece of work, unlike the tap.

Three things were tried and dropped. A pencil button at the end of the line is the same trap in a smaller target, and never says which key. A coach mark shown once teaches the person who reads it that day and nobody who comes back in a month, and it needs somewhere to remember it has been shown. Nothing at all leaves the phone with no way in, which is the surface the drawer was for.

Where it sits on a card

The note belongs in the card, because the card leaves

Answer a card and it tilts, takes a wash of the verdict, and is thrown off the side while the next one deals into its place. Everything that belongs to the word has to go with it. A note under the card is left sitting there while the word it was written about flies away — and for a beat it reads as a note about the card arriving. Switch the demo to under the card and answer one; the flight is the argument.

It is the same answer from the other side, too. The card is the only thing on the screen that says which word is being asked about, so a line outside it is equidistant between the word, the progress rule and the two answers — it could belong to any of them. Inside, under the definition, there is nothing else it could be about. And it costs no height either way: every card in a walk can be noted, so the line is never empty and never reserved for nothing.

Which is also why keeping a note does not answer the card. It first did — a note being a stronger yes than the → — and the card flew off the instant the last word was typed, so the note was never once seen where it lives. Nothing on a card is decided until it is answered: Enter means keep these words, the drawer closes, and the note is sitting under the definition of the word it is about. Then the two answers still mean what they say. A typo is still fixable, too, which it was not when the card left with it.

In the code that means FeelingCard and NeedCard take an optional note region rather than FeelingPrompt laying one out beside them — a small change, and the only one that keeps the flight honest, since the leaving card is a copy of the same component.

Which words carry one

A badge that says there is writing here

A note is invisible until you land on the word, which is no good in a grid of twenty-eight or in a category card that lists what you picked. Three ways to say it, switched live.

The icon is the one worth the width. The dot and the rule are free — paint inside padding the pill already has — but both read as a rendering artefact until someone tells you what they mean. The glyph says writing on its own, and because a noted word is always a marked one, the badge never appears on a word that was not already drawn differently: a row can only grow when you add a note to it, by about one character, and never under the finger that is tapping something else.

What it touches

Where a note has to live

A note is picked state, so it belongs beside visited in the two picker machines and has to survive the round trip through the fence in the note — which is the real design question here, not the keystrokes. Now that a note runs to several lines, it cannot ride in a parenthesis on the category bullet. 

- Angry: irate, livid
    - irate: only about the meeting, not about him.
      It ran an hour over and I never got to speak.

An indented bullet under the category, keyed by the word, with wrapped lines indented past it. The block still reads with the plugin off, the category bullets are untouched — so resolve.ts keeps working out the inventory from the words alone — and a note may contain any character at all, which the parenthesis form could not offer. The cost is that the parser gains a second kind of line, and a note whose word is not in the bullet above it is one more way for a block to fail to read.
